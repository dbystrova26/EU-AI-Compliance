## Transition Note from EU AI Act Lab

This GDPR audit carries forward the A&R Artist Intelligence Agent, which was classified as **limited risk** (with a flag to resolve Annex III §4 applicability) in the EU AI Act peer audit conducted by Julian Granados. The same system is the most interesting case for GDPR purposes because:

1. It processes artist names combined with AI-generated scores, reasoning narratives, and recommendation decisions — a combination that almost certainly constitutes personal data under GDPR Article 4(1), even though the brief initially characterised the data as non-personal
2. The Google Sheets log retains this data with no stated retention limits or access controls
3. The system involves automated scoring (not final automated decisions) about identifiable natural persons
4. Third-party AI vendors (Anthropic, OpenAI) and data source APIs (Spotify, Last.fm, NewsAPI, YouTube) are in the stack, raising processor and transfer questions

The AI Act peer audit identified GDPR compliance for the evaluation log (Finding 2) as the most significant parallel legal issue requiring immediate action.

## Phase 1: Fact Pattern

**Who is the client?** Believe is a global independent digital music distribution company headquartered in Paris, France. It operates across multiple markets including the EU, UK, and internationally. It has several hundred employees and works with tens of thousands of independent artists globally.

**What personal data is processed?** The system processes publicly available professional data about independent artists: artist name, streaming metrics (monthly listeners, weekly listeners, playcount, velocity), press article counts and outlet names, YouTube channel statistics, and genre information. Critically, the system also generates and stores AI-produced outputs about each artist: a score (0–100), a decision (SIGN / WATCH / PASS), a plain-language reasoning paragraph, and a full 9-section research report. These outputs are stored in a Google Sheets log and in Airtable/local file storage. The data volume is moderate — every evaluation run creates a new record. Artists evaluated are predominantly EU-based independent musicians, though some are based in other jurisdictions.

**Who are the data subjects?** Independent artists evaluated by the system. These are identifiable natural persons — their names are the primary input, and their public professional activity forms the basis of the analysis. They are not Believe employees or applicants; they are external individuals being assessed for a potential commercial relationship without their knowledge or involvement.

**Where are the data subjects located?** Mixed — EU/EEA (majority), UK, US, and other territories depending on which artists are evaluated. EU/EEA artists are the primary concern for GDPR purposes.

**Who are the vendors in the stack?**
- **Anthropic** (US-based) — Claude API used for major label check, reasoning synthesis, and full report generation
- **OpenAI** (US-based) — text-embedding-3-small used for Pinecone vector similarity
- **Pinecone** (US-based) — vector database storing 25 Believe roster artist profiles
- **Google** (US-based) — Google Sheets for evaluation log; NewsAPI for press data
- **Spotify** (US/Sweden) — streaming data source
- **Last.fm** (UK-based, CBS Interactive) — streaming fallback data source
- **YouTube / Google** (US-based) — channel statistics
- **Render.com** (US-based) — hosting platform for the FastAPI application

**What does the AI system actually do with the data?** The system takes an artist name, automatically retrieves publicly available professional metrics from four external APIs, runs two AI agents (LangChain triage and LangGraph research), generates a score and recommendation, produces a written reasoning explanation and a 9-section report, logs all outputs to Google Sheets, and sends automated Slack alerts for SIGN and WATCH decisions. Artists are not informed this processing is taking place. No artist-facing communication is generated by the system.

**Is there automated decision-making with legal or similarly significant effects?** The system produces automated scores and recommendations that influence whether Believe will pursue signing an artist. This is not a final automated decision in the Article 22 sense — a human A&R manager reviews and approves all outputs before any contact is made. However, the scoring and recommendation have a similarly significant effect on the artist's professional opportunities: a PASS recommendation means the artist will not be considered by Believe through this pipeline.

### Section B — Risk and Rights

**Are any special-category data present or inferable from the outputs (Article 9)?**

Not directly collected. However, genre data (e.g. "afrobeats," "french-rap," "bollywood") combined with artist name could in some cases allow inference of ethnic or national origin, which is special-category data under Article 9(1). The system does not collect or process health, political opinion, or religious data. The inference risk is low but not zero — particularly for artists whose genre is closely associated with a specific ethnic or cultural background. This should be flagged for legal review.

**Is there automated decision-making with legal or similarly significant effects (Article 22)?**

The system produces automated scoring and recommendation outputs that have a similarly significant professional effect on artists — a PASS means Believe will not pursue signing through this pipeline. However, Article 22 applies to fully automated decisions "without any human involvement." The Approve Signing workflow requires a human A&R manager to review and confirm every recommendation before any action is taken. If this human review step is genuinely meaningful (not rubber-stamping), Article 22 does not apply. If in practice the human always follows the agent's recommendation without independent review, the Article 22 safeguard question reopens. Safeguard recommended: document the human review as a substantive step, not a formality.

**Is a DPIA required?**

Using the EDPB's nine criteria:

1. **Evaluation or scoring** ✓ — the system scores artists 0–100 and classifies them
2. **Automated decision-making with legal effects** — partial (see above); human review is present
3. **Systematic monitoring** — not continuous monitoring of behaviour
4. **Sensitive data or data of a highly personal nature** — low risk (professional public data); minor inference risk for ethnicity via genre
5. **Data processed on a large scale** — moderate scale; not large-scale in EDPB terms
6. **Matching or combining datasets** — ✓ yes — combines Spotify, Last.fm, NewsAPI, YouTube, and AI-generated outputs about the same individual
7. **Data concerning vulnerable data subjects** — not applicable
8. **Innovative use or applying new technological solutions** — ✓ yes — AI agents, RAG, vector similarity search applied to A&R decisions
9. **Data transfer across borders** — ✓ yes — multiple US vendors

**Conclusion:** At least three EDPB criteria are met (scoring/evaluation, combining datasets, innovative technology + international transfer). **A DPIA is recommended** before production deployment.

**What data subject friction points are most likely?**

- **Right of access (Article 15):** An artist could submit a Subject Access Request asking what data Believe holds about them. Believe would need to disclose the score, reasoning, and report — which could be commercially sensitive and operationally awkward.
- **Right to erasure (Article 17):** An artist could request deletion from the Google Sheets log and any stored reports. The legitimate interests basis does not create an absolute right to object but the request must be assessed.
- **Right to object to profiling (Article 21):** Artists have a right to object to processing based on legitimate interests, including profiling. Believe would need a documented process to handle such objections.
- **Right of explanation for automated decisions:** Even if Article 22 does not strictly apply, best practice (and the AI Act's transparency principles) support providing artists with an explanation of how the system works if asked.

**What is the controller / processor split?**

- **Controller:** Believe — determines the purposes and means of processing artist data for A&R evaluation
- **Processors:** Anthropic (processes artist data to generate reasoning), OpenAI (processes artist data to generate embeddings), Pinecone (stores and searches vector data), Render.com (hosts the application and all data in transit), Google (Sheets log storage)
- **Independent controllers:** Spotify, Last.fm, NewsAPI, YouTube — Believe queries their APIs but they independently control their own data. They are not processors for Believe; they are data sources. No DPA required with them, but their ToS must permit the intended use.

**Is a DPA needed with any vendor?**

Yes — DPAs are required with all processors who receive or process personal data on Believe's behalf:
- **Anthropic** — artist names and data are sent to Claude API; DPA required
- **OpenAI** — artist data sent for embedding generation; DPA required
- **Pinecone** — stores vector representations of data; DPA required
- **Render.com** — hosts application processing all personal data; DPA required
- **Google** — Google Sheets stores the evaluation log containing personal data; DPA required (Google Workspace DPA likely already available)

### Section C — Law Stacking

**AI Act cross-check:**
The system was classified as **limited risk** in the EU AI Act audit, with a flag to resolve whether Annex III §4 (employment/self-employment) applies. The AI Act adds obligations GDPR does not cover: technical documentation (Article 11), transparency to users (Article 13), human oversight design (Article 14), and accuracy/robustness (Article 15). The GDPR DPIA requirement and the AI Act human oversight requirement overlap in practice — both require formalising the review workflow. Running both compliance tracks in parallel is necessary and efficient.

**ePrivacy check:**
The system does not involve cookies, tracking pixels, or device-level data in the traditional sense. The web interface at ar-agent-zkjw.onrender.com uses no cookies for tracking or analytics purposes. ePrivacy Directive obligations are minimal. If the interface were to add any analytics cookies or session tracking in future, consent would be required. No ePrivacy override applies to the current design.

**Data Act check:**
Not applicable. The system does not involve IoT connected products, cloud switching, or infrastructure data sharing in the Data Act sense. The Data Act is not relevant to this scenario.

**Bottom line: Go with conditions.**

The A&R Agent can proceed toward production, but three compliance steps must be completed before any live deployment processing real artist evaluations. The system's current design has meaningful strengths — genuine human review before any artist contact, internal-only access, and publicly available source data — but the GDPR compliance infrastructure around the evaluation log and vendor relationships is underdeveloped for a production system.

**Top three actions, in sequence:**

**1. Establish Data Processing Agreements with all processors before any data flows (immediately).**
Anthropic, OpenAI, Pinecone, and Render.com all receive personal data (artist names and AI-generated assessments) in the course of running the system. GDPR Article 28 requires a written DPA with each processor before processing begins. Most major vendors offer standard DPAs — this is an administrative step, but it must be completed first. Google Workspace DPA likely already exists; verify it covers the Sheets log use case.

**2. Conduct a DPIA before first processing run (before production deployment).**
The system meets at least three EDPB DPIA trigger criteria: it scores and evaluates individuals, it combines multiple data sources about the same person, and it applies innovative AI technology with cross-border data transfers to US vendors. A DPIA does not have to be long — a structured assessment of the processing, its necessity, the risks to artists, and the mitigations in place (human review, public data only, internal access) is sufficient. Given the EU-to-US transfers, the DPIA should also document the transfer mechanisms (SCCs or DPF certification) for each US vendor.

**3. Define and document a data retention policy for the evaluation log and reports (within 30 days of deployment).**
The Google Sheets log and stored research reports currently have no stated retention limit. Under GDPR Article 5(1)(e), personal data must not be kept longer than necessary. Recommend a 12-month retention period for the evaluation log, with anonymisation or deletion thereafter unless a business relationship with the artist has commenced. Reports should follow the same cycle. Access to the log should be restricted to authorised A&R staff only.

**Residual risks (even if all recommendations are followed):**

1. **Artist awareness gap.** Artists whose data is processed have no notice of this activity. The legitimate interests basis does not require consent, but it does create a right to object. If an artist discovers the system and submits a Subject Access Request or an objection, Believe will need to respond within statutory timeframes and may be required to disclose commercially sensitive scoring outputs. A process for handling these requests should be prepared even if rarely needed.

2. **Annex III §4 reclassification risk.** If EU AI Act counsel determines that Believe's distribution agreements constitute access to self-employment under Annex III §4, the system becomes high-risk and the GDPR DPIA must be revisited alongside a full AI Act conformity assessment. This risk cannot be eliminated without the legal opinion recommended in the EU AI Act audit — it is a parallel and linked obligation.

3. **US vendor transfer risk (post-Schrems II environment).** Even with SCCs in place, transfers to US vendors operating under US surveillance law (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Render) carry residual transfer risk. The EU-US Data Privacy Framework reduces but does not eliminate this risk. Any future regulatory challenge to the DPF (as happened with Privacy Shield) could require rapid remediation of the transfer mechanisms.

---

**Caveats:** This memo is not a legal opinion and does not constitute GDPR compliance certification. It is a first-pass advisory note for planning purposes. The DPIA, DPA review, and Annex III legal opinion should all involve qualified legal counsel before production deployment.

## Reinforce: DPIA Outline

**Processing activity:** AI-generated scoring, recommendation, and report generation for artist evaluation

**Description of processing:**
The system collects publicly available professional metrics about named independent artists from four external APIs, processes this data through two AI agents (LangChain triage, LangGraph research), generates a score (0–100), a SIGN/WATCH/PASS recommendation, a reasoning paragraph, and a 9-section research report. All outputs are stored in Google Sheets and on Render-hosted file storage. Artist names and AI-generated assessments are transmitted to three US-based processors (Anthropic, OpenAI, Pinecone) in the course of processing. Artists are not notified.

**Necessity and proportionality:**
The processing is necessary for Believe's legitimate A&R function — manual research at scale is not feasible. Publicly available data is used rather than private data. A human review step precedes any commercial action. The processing is proportionate to the business purpose, provided retention is limited and access controlled.

**Risk identification:**
- Artists are profiled and scored without notice or consent
- AI-generated assessments may contain inaccuracies that persist in the evaluation log
- US vendor transfers expose data to potential third-country access
- Genre data may allow inference of ethnic origin (special-category risk, low probability)
- No current mechanism for artists to exercise data subject rights

**Proposed mitigation:**
- DPAs with all US processors including SCCs for transfer mechanism
- 12-month retention limit with access controls on Google Sheets log
- Human review workflow documented as mandatory gate
- Process for handling Subject Access Requests and objections
- Accuracy disclaimer in reports noting proxy metrics and estimated data

## Reinforce: DPA Clause

**Vendor:** Anthropic (Claude API)

**Clause wanted:** Sub-processor restriction with prior written consent requirement.

Anthropic's standard API terms permit them to use sub-processors. For this use case, artist names and AI-generated assessments about identifiable individuals are transmitted to Claude. Believe needs a clause requiring Anthropic to: (a) list all sub-processors who may process Believe's data; (b) notify Believe of any new sub-processor additions with sufficient notice to object; (c) ensure all sub-processors are bound by data protection obligations equivalent to those in the DPA.

**Why:** Without this clause, Believe cannot maintain visibility over where artist personal data flows once it enters Anthropic's infrastructure, making GDPR Article 28(2) compliance impossible to verify.
