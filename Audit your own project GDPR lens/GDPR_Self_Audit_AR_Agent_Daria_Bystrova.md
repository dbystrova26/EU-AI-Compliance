## Data Processing Brief

This brief is written for a Data Protection Officer reviewing the system for the first time.

**What personal data does the system process?**

The system processes data about independent music artists who are identifiable natural persons. Specifically:

- **Artist name** — direct identifier, entered by the user as the primary input
- **Streaming metrics** — monthly listeners, weekly listeners, playcount, estimated velocity — retrieved from Spotify and Last.fm APIs. These are professional activity metrics about a named individual.
- **Press coverage data** — article count, outlet names, publication dates — retrieved from NewsAPI. Relates to named individuals' public activity.
- **Video metrics** — YouTube channel subscriber count, total views, upload frequency, last upload date — linked to a named artist's channel.
- **AI-generated outputs** — score (0–100), decision (SIGN / WATCH / PASS), reasoning paragraph, and a 9-section research report. These are generated by the system about the named artist and constitute profiling outputs.
- **Genre tags** — retrieved from Last.fm. In some cases genre may allow inference of ethnic or cultural background (e.g. "afrobeats," "bollywood," "french-rap").

**Where does the data come from?**

- Artist name: entered manually by an internal Believe user
- Streaming and genre data: Spotify Web API and Last.fm API (publicly available professional data)
- Press data: NewsAPI (publicly available articles)
- Video data: YouTube Data API v3 (publicly available channel statistics)
- AI-generated outputs: Anthropic Claude API (reasoning, report synthesis) and OpenAI API (embeddings for roster similarity)

**What is the data used for?**

1. Triage evaluation — scoring the artist across five dimensions and producing a SIGN/WATCH/PASS recommendation
2. Research report generation — producing a 9-section analysis report for SIGN, WATCH, and PASS decisions
3. Operational logging — recording all evaluations in Google Sheets for A&R team reference
4. Internal notification — sending Slack alerts to the A&R team for SIGN and WATCH decisions

**Who processes it?**

- The system itself (FastAPI application hosted on Render.com, US)
- Anthropic Claude API (US) — receives artist name and retrieved data to generate reasoning and reports
- OpenAI API (US) — receives artist data to generate vector embeddings for roster similarity search
- Pinecone (US) — stores and searches vector representations
- Google (US) — Google Sheets stores the evaluation log; NewsAPI provides press data
- Render.com (US) — hosts the application; all data passes through it in transit

**Where is the data stored and processed?**

All processing and storage occurs on US-based infrastructure: Render (hosting), Anthropic (inference), OpenAI (embeddings), Pinecone (vector storage), Google Sheets (log). No EU-based processing infrastructure is used. All data flows cross the EU border if data subjects are EU-based artists.

**Does the system make or assist in decisions that affect people?**

Yes. The system produces automated scores and SIGN/WATCH/PASS recommendations that directly influence whether Believe will pursue a commercial relationship with an artist. A PASS recommendation means the artist will not be contacted through this pipeline. A SIGN recommendation leads to the A&R manager initiating contact. A human A&R manager reviews all outputs before any action is taken via the "Approve Signing" workflow in the Watchlist & Universe tab. The human review is genuine but its operational enforcement is not technically mandatory.

**Does the system process personal data at all?**

Yes — explicitly confirmed. Artist names combined with AI-generated scores, reasoning narratives, and recommendation decisions constitute personal data under GDPR Article 4(1). Artists are identifiable natural persons. The initial project brief characterised this data as non-personal "professional/commercial information" — that characterisation is too narrow and is corrected here.

**Legitimate Interests Assessment (LIA) — primary processing:**

1. **Is the interest legitimate?** Yes. Identifying and signing independent artists is Believe's core commercial activity. A&R evaluation at scale using publicly available data is a reasonable and proportionate business practice.

2. **Is the processing necessary?** Yes. Manual research at the required scale is not feasible. Automated retrieval and AI-assisted analysis is the least intrusive method to achieve the business purpose. Source data is limited to publicly available professional metrics.

3. **Does the individual's interest override?** Probably not, with caveats. Artists are public figures acting in their professional capacity. The data processed is their public professional activity, not private life data. Processing does not expose them to harm, discrimination, or reputational damage beyond the inherent commercial risk of evaluation. However, artists have no notice of processing and no easy way to exercise their right to object. This is the weakest part of the balancing test and should be addressed with a process for handling objections.

## Phase 4: Risk and Rights Analysis

**Special category data (Article 9):**

No special-category data is directly collected. However, genre tags retrieved from Last.fm (e.g. "afrobeats," "bollywood," "french-rap," "punjabi") could in some cases allow inference of an artist's ethnic or cultural origin, which is special-category data under Article 9(1)(a). The inference risk is low — genre is not a reliable proxy for ethnic origin and the system does not explicitly derive ethnicity — but it is not zero. No Article 9 condition has been identified or documented. Legal review is recommended to confirm whether the inference risk is sufficient to trigger Article 9 obligations, and if so, which condition (likely Article 9(2)(e) — manifestly made public — or Article 9(2)(f) — legal claims) could apply.

**Automated decision-making (Article 22):**

The system produces automated scores and SIGN/WATCH/PASS recommendations about identifiable individuals that have a similarly significant professional effect — particularly PASS decisions that exclude artists from Believe's pipeline. Article 22 applies to fully automated decisions "without any human involvement." The system includes a human review step via the "Approve Signing" workflow in the Watchlist & Universe tab. If this review is genuinely meaningful and not a rubber stamp, Article 22 does not strictly apply. Safeguards in place: human override field, manual approval required before artist contact, agent decision framed as a suggestion. Gap: the human review is not technically enforced — a manager could theoretically act without completing it. Recommendation: formalise the review as a documented, mandatory workflow gate with an audit trail.

**DPIA trigger assessment (EDPB nine criteria):**

1. ✓ **Evaluation or scoring of people** — the system scores artists 0–100 and classifies them
2. ~ **Automated decision-making with significant effects** — partially; human review present but not technically enforced
3. ✗ **Systematic monitoring** — not continuous behavioural monitoring
4. ~ **Sensitive data** — low-probability inference risk for ethnic origin via genre tags
5. ✗ **Large-scale processing** — moderate scale; not EDPB large-scale threshold
6. ✓ **Matching or combining datasets** — combines Spotify, Last.fm, NewsAPI, YouTube, and AI-generated outputs about the same individual
7. ✗ **Vulnerable data subjects** — not applicable
8. ✓ **Innovative technology** — AI agents, RAG, LangGraph, vector similarity search applied to commercial decisions
9. ✓ **Cross-border transfer** — all processing occurs on US infrastructure; EU-to-US transfers for all data flows

**Conclusion: Four EDPB criteria met. A DPIA is required before production deployment.**

**Data subject rights friction:**

- **Right of access (Article 15):** An artist could submit a Subject Access Request. Believe would need to provide the score, reasoning paragraph, and research report — which are commercially sensitive internal documents. No process currently exists to handle this. Must be designed before deployment.
- **Right to erasure (Article 17):** An artist could request deletion from the Google Sheets log and stored reports. The legitimate interests basis does not create an absolute erasure right, but the request must be formally assessed and responded to within one month. No erasure workflow exists.
- **Right to object to profiling (Article 21):** Artists have the right to object to processing based on legitimate interests. The system has no mechanism to receive, record, or respond to such objections. A documented process is required.
- **Right to explanation:** Not an absolute right under GDPR but expected as best practice for automated scoring systems. The system's reasoning paragraph provides a partial explanation but is not designed with data subject access in mind.

## Phase 5: Law Stacking Check

**AI Act cross-check:**
The system was classified as **limited risk** with a flag to resolve Annex III §4 (access to self-employment) before confirming. The AI Act adds obligations GDPR does not cover: technical documentation (Article 11), transparency to deployers (Article 13), human oversight design (Article 14), and accuracy/robustness requirements (Article 15). The GDPR DPIA and the AI Act human oversight formalisation overlap in practice — both require documenting and enforcing the human review step. Both compliance tracks must run in parallel.

**ePrivacy check:**
The web interface at ar-agent-zkjw.onrender.com does not use cookies, tracking pixels, or device-level data collection. The system does not intercept or process the content of electronic communications in the ePrivacy sense. ePrivacy obligations are not triggered by the current design. If analytics or session cookies are added to the interface in future, prior consent would be required.

**Data Act check:**
Not applicable. The system does not involve IoT connected products, cloud switching, or infrastructure data sharing covered by the Data Act.

**Bottom line: Proceed with conditions.**

The A&R Agent processes personal data about identifiable independent artists and cannot be deployed in a production environment without completing three compliance steps. The system's design has genuine strengths — human review before any artist contact, publicly available source data, and internal-only access — but the GDPR infrastructure is underdeveloped for a production system. None of the gaps are technically difficult to close, but they are legally required.

**Top three actions, in sequence:**

**1. Establish Data Processing Agreements with Anthropic, OpenAI, Pinecone, Render.com, and Google before any personal data flows (immediately, before any live evaluation of EU-based artists).**
Every time the system runs, artist names and AI-generated assessments about identifiable individuals are transmitted to five US-based processors. GDPR Article 28 makes a written DPA with each processor a legal requirement — not a best practice. Most vendors offer standard DPAs; this is an administrative task but it must be completed first, as it gates everything else. Each DPA must also include Standard Contractual Clauses or confirm DPF certification as the international transfer mechanism for EU-to-US data flows.

**2. Conduct a DPIA before first production run.**
The system triggers four EDPB DPIA criteria: it scores and classifies individuals, it combines multiple public datasets about the same person, it uses innovative AI technology for commercial decisions, and it transfers all data to US processors. A DPIA does not have to be lengthy — a structured assessment of the processing activity, necessity and proportionality, identified risks to artists, and proposed mitigations is sufficient. The DPIA should document the legitimate interests assessment, the human review safeguard, and the transfer mechanisms. It should also address the low-probability inference risk for ethnic origin via genre tags.

**3. Define a data retention policy and access controls for the Google Sheets evaluation log (within 30 days of deployment).**
The log currently retains all evaluation records indefinitely with no stated access controls. GDPR Article 5(1)(e) requires that personal data not be kept longer than necessary for the stated purpose. Recommended: 12-month active retention, then deletion or anonymisation, unless a business relationship with the artist has commenced. The log must be restricted to named authorised A&R staff — not accessible to anyone with the link.

**Residual risks:**

1. **Artist awareness and objection risk.** Artists whose data is processed have no notice of this activity and no mechanism to object. While the legitimate interests basis does not require consent, artists retain the right to object under Article 21 and the right of access under Article 15. An artist who discovers the system exists and submits a Subject Access Request will require a response within one month — including disclosure of their score, reasoning, and report. A handling process must be designed even if rarely needed.

2. **Annex III §4 reclassification.** If EU AI Act counsel determines that Believe's distribution agreements constitute access to self-employment under Annex III §4, the system becomes high-risk under the AI Act. This would require a conformity assessment and would likely require the GDPR DPIA to be revisited and expanded. This risk cannot be eliminated without the legal opinion recommended in the EU AI Act audit — it is a linked parallel obligation.

3. **US vendor transfer instability.** All five processors are US-based. Transfers rely on SCCs and/or DPF certification. The DPF has only been in place since July 2023 and its predecessor (Privacy Shield) was invalidated by the CJEU in 2020. A future legal challenge to the DPF could require rapid renegotiation of transfer mechanisms across all vendor relationships simultaneously. This is a structural risk that cannot be fully mitigated through design choices alone.

---

**What this memo is not:** This is not a legal opinion, a DPIA, or a GDPR compliance certification. It is a first-pass advisory assessment for planning purposes. The client must engage qualified legal counsel and, where applicable, a Data Protection Officer before relying on this assessment for compliance decisions or regulatory filings.

## Reinforce: TBD Legal Review — Unpacking the LIA

The item marked TBD — legal review most requiring preparation is the **Legitimate Interests Assessment for sending artist personal data to Anthropic and OpenAI**.

What a lawyer would need to know:
- The exact data transmitted to each API (artist name + retrieved metrics + existing AI-generated content)
- Whether Anthropic and OpenAI use API inputs to train their models (if yes, this creates a purpose limitation problem — data sent for inference is being used for training, which is a different purpose the artist has not been notified of)
- The current DPF certification status of Anthropic and OpenAI (determines whether SCCs are needed as the transfer mechanism)
- Whether Believe has signed Anthropic's and OpenAI's enterprise data processing addenda, which typically include model-training opt-outs

Information to prepare in advance: a one-page summary of exactly what data fields are sent in each API call, confirmation of whether model training opt-outs have been activated, and copies of the vendor DPA/DPA addendum terms.
