## Phase 1: Read and Annotate — Key Observations

Reading the system brief and README, the following elements are most relevant to the compliance assessment:

- The system generates **public-facing content** (Instagram captions, LinkedIn posts, carousel posts, reels) on behalf of a named individual practitioner (Julia G)
- The content relates to **psychological and transformational topics** — a sensitive domain
- Content is generated from **copyrighted or proprietary source material** (MSP books, PDFs, transcripts) embedded in a Pinecone vector store
- The system includes **Tavily internet research** which scrapes and summarises third-party web content
- Outputs are **drafts saved to Airtable for human review** — not published automatically
- The system **interacts with end users indirectly**: the practitioner reviews drafts and may publish them; the final audience is the practitioner's clients and followers
- The system is **scheduled to run autonomously every two days** without a human triggering each run
- No disclosure mechanism is described for audiences who consume the published content

## Phase 3: Clarifying Questions Log

**Question 1 — Who owns the MSP source documents in Pinecone?**  
*Why it matters:* The system retrieves and generates content grounded in uploaded PDFs, books, and transcripts. If these are copyrighted works owned by the MSP institution or third-party publishers, embedding them in a vector database and using them to generate commercial content may infringe copyright. This does not affect the AI Act risk tier but is a parallel legal risk.  
*Provisional assumption:* The client (Julia G) has permission or licence to use this material, or it is her own proprietary content.

**Question 2 — Will the published content include any disclosure that it was AI-assisted or AI-generated?**  
*Why it matters:* Article 50(1) of the AI Act requires that content generated by AI be labelled as such unless it is obvious from context that it is AI-generated. Social media posts and newsletters do not make this obvious. The README describes a human review step but says nothing about disclosure to the audience.  
*Provisional assumption:* No disclosure is currently planned, which would constitute an Article 50 gap.

**Question 3 — What is the nature of the psychological content being generated?**  
*Why it matters:* The system generates content on "psychological and transformational concepts" intended to attract clients to a therapeutic/coaching practice. If this content makes health claims, implies therapeutic efficacy, or could influence vulnerable individuals' decisions about their mental health, it may be subject to additional regulation beyond the AI Act (e.g. healthcare communications rules, consumer protection law).  
*Provisional assumption:* The content is educational/promotional rather than clinical advice, but this should be verified.

**Question 4 — Is the Tavily web research tool scraping and reproducing content from third-party websites?**  
*Why it matters:* If the system summarises or paraphrases third-party web content and that content is included in published posts, this raises copyright questions separate from the AI Act.  
*Provisional assumption:* Tavily provides summaries and context rather than verbatim reproduction, but the extent of reproduction is unclear from the README.

**Question 5 — What happens if the AI generates content that is factually incorrect about MSP or mental health?**  
*Why it matters:* The README acknowledges that "unsupported claims should be avoided" — implying the system could produce them. In a psychologically sensitive domain, inaccurate content could harm the practitioner's clients. The README does not describe any accuracy checks beyond the human review step.  
*Provisional assumption:* The human review step is the only safeguard, which may be insufficient if the reviewer is not evaluating factual accuracy systematically.

## Phase 4: Audit Report

### Section 1: System Summary

The Mental Space Psychology AI Storytelling Agent is a content generation tool built for a practitioner named Julia G, who works in the Mental Space Psychology field. The system takes a topic as input, retrieves relevant information from a proprietary vector database of MSP source documents, enriches it with Tavily web research, and uses OpenAI to generate multi-platform content drafts including Instagram captions, LinkedIn posts, carousel outlines, and reel ideas. Drafts are saved to Airtable for human review and email notifications are sent via n8n. The system runs on a two-day automated schedule. No content is published automatically — a human review step precedes publication.

---

### Section 2: Risk Classification

**Tier: Limited risk — Article 50 transparency obligations apply**

The system does not fall under Annex III high-risk categories because it generates content drafts for human review rather than making decisions about individuals. However, it clearly falls under Article 50(1): it uses an AI system to generate content that a natural person (Julia G) will publish to her audience. Unless the AI origin is obvious from context — which it is not for social media posts — the recipients must be informed that the content is AI-generated.

There is a secondary ambiguity: the content concerns psychological and mental wellbeing, a sensitive domain. While this does not trigger Annex III on its own, it elevates the significance of the Article 50 gap and the accuracy/harm risk.

### Section 4: Compliance Findings

---

**Finding 1 — Article 50 AI Content Disclosure**  
**Severity: Significant**

**Description:** Article 50(1) of the EU AI Act requires that when an AI system generates content that a natural person publishes or distributes, recipients must be informed that the content was AI-generated, unless it is obvious from context. The system generates Instagram captions, LinkedIn posts, newsletters, and other public-facing content. Social media posts from a practitioner are not obviously AI-generated from context. The README describes a human review step in Airtable but contains no mention of any disclosure mechanism for the final audience.

**Recommended action:** The deployer (Julia G) should add a standard disclosure to all AI-assisted content before publication — for example, "Created with AI assistance" as a footer or hashtag. The builder should add a recommended disclosure template to the Airtable schema (e.g. a pre-filled "AI Disclosure" field) and document this requirement in the README and any user guide.

**Escalation needed:** No for basic disclosure design. Yes — legal review recommended — to confirm whether the disclosure format satisfies Article 50 requirements in the specific jurisdictions where Julia G publishes.

---

**Finding 2 — No formal transparency information for the deployer (Article 13)**  
**Severity: Significant**

**Description:** Article 13 requires that AI systems placed on the market include clear information enabling deployers to understand the system's capabilities, limitations, and how to use it responsibly. The README is detailed but written as a developer/technical document, not a deployer-facing transparency notice. A non-technical user (Julia G) reading the README would struggle to understand: what the system can get wrong, what the human review step should specifically check, or what happens when Tavily returns low-quality web content.

**Recommended action:** Add a one-page "User Guide for Deployers" to the repository covering: what the system does and does not do, known limitations (dependency on source document quality, Tavily result variability, risk of factually incorrect psychological claims), what the human reviewer should specifically check before approving a draft, and the Article 50 disclosure obligation.

**Escalation needed:** No.

---

**Finding 3 — Accuracy in psychologically sensitive domain (Article 15 and ethical risk)**  
**Severity: Significant**

**Description:** The system generates content about psychological and mental wellbeing topics. The README explicitly acknowledges that "unsupported claims should be avoided" but the only safeguard is a general human review step. The Airtable schema includes a "Reviewer Notes" field and a status workflow, but no checklist or structured criteria for what a reviewer should verify. In a sensitive domain, a reviewer without a structured accuracy protocol may miss factually incorrect or potentially harmful claims.

**Recommended action:** Add a structured review checklist to the Airtable workflow — at minimum: (1) does the content accurately reflect MSP principles? (2) does it make any health claims that could be misleading? (3) does the source reference exist and support the content? This checklist should be documented in the user guide and the README.

**Escalation needed:** No for the checklist. Potentially yes — if the content makes any direct claims about psychological efficacy or therapeutic outcomes, consumer protection law and healthcare communications regulation may apply, requiring specialist legal advice.

---

**Finding 4 — Copyright and IP risk from RAG source material**  
**Severity: Significant** (parallel legal issue, not AI Act)

**Description:** The system embeds MSP books, PDFs, and transcripts into Pinecone for retrieval and uses them to generate commercial content. If any of these documents are copyrighted works not owned by Julia G or the client, their use in a commercial RAG pipeline may constitute copyright infringement under EU law (Copyright Directive 2019/790). This is not an AI Act issue but is a material legal risk that the builder and deployer should address before production use.

**Recommended action:** Document in the README that all source documents must be owned by or licensed to the deployer before ingestion. Add a step to the onboarding process requiring the deployer to confirm they have rights to use each document.

**Escalation needed:** Yes — legal review recommended to confirm that the use of the source documents in a RAG pipeline is covered by any existing licences or permissions.

---

**Finding 5 — Autonomous scheduling without human trigger (human oversight risk)**  
**Severity: Minor**

**Description:** The system is designed to run automatically every two days without a human initiating each run. While drafts are not published automatically, the automated generation means that content is being produced and stored without the deployer's active involvement in initiating it. If the system generates problematic content on a cycle when the deployer is unavailable or not checking Airtable, that content sits in the database unreviewed. The current design relies on email notification as the only alert mechanism.

**Recommended action:** Consider adding a confirmation step before generation runs (e.g. an n8n approval node requiring the deployer to confirm the next topic before content is generated) or at minimum a time-to-review policy requiring all drafts to be reviewed within 24 hours of generation. Document the review expectation in the user guide.

**Escalation needed:** No.

---

### Section 5: Overall Recommendation

**Proceed with conditions**

The system has no blocking findings under the EU AI Act — it does not fall under prohibited practices and is not clearly high-risk. However, three conditions should be met before any commercial deployment:

1. **Article 50 disclosure** must be designed and implemented before any AI-generated content is published to Julia G's audience.
2. **A deployer-facing user guide** must be produced explaining the system's limitations and what the human review step should specifically check, particularly for psychologically sensitive claims.
3. **Copyright clearance** for all source documents embedded in Pinecone must be confirmed before production use.

These are practical steps that can be completed without external specialists, with the exception of the copyright review and the Article 50 legal confirmation, which are recommended but not strictly blocking.

**Joint closing note (draft — to be confirmed with Julian):**

> *Auditing your own system and auditing someone else's reveal fundamentally different blind spots. The builder tends to see what the system does correctly and explains design choices from the inside. The external auditor sees what the system does not say — what is missing, unstated, or assumed. In this case, the most significant gap (Article 50 disclosure to the end audience) was not visible from the architecture description alone; it only became apparent by following the content all the way to its final destination — the practitioner's social media followers. Documentation that is thorough about how the system works can still leave compliance gaps about what happens after the system runs.*
